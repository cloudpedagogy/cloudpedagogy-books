# Introduction

## 

## Purpose of This Book

### Why agency is the central question in AI-supported work

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly embedded in everyday
work, discussions often focus on what AI can do: how powerful systems
have become, how quickly they are improving, and how widely they can be
deployed. These discussions are not unimportant. Capability,
performance, and scale all matter. However, they frequently obscure a
more fundamental question---one that determines whether AI use
strengthens or undermines human systems over time.

That question is **agency**.

At its core, agency concerns who decides, who is responsible, and who
remains accountable when AI systems are introduced into human activity.
In AI-supported work, agency is not a philosophical abstraction; it is a
practical condition that shapes decision-making, professional judgement,
ethical responsibility, and institutional trust. When agency is clear
and well supported, AI can augment human capability. When it is
ambiguous or eroded, even technically impressive systems can produce
fragile, risky, or harmful outcomes.

This book starts from the premise that **human--AI co-agency is the
central capability challenge of AI adoption**. Questions of ethics,
governance, innovation, and impact all ultimately depend on how agency
is distributed and exercised. Without clarity about who retains
authority, how decisions are made, and where responsibility lies, other
capability efforts struggle to take root.

In many organisations, agency is treated implicitly rather than
intentionally. AI tools are introduced to improve efficiency or insight,
but little attention is paid to how these tools reshape decision
boundaries, influence judgement, or redistribute responsibility. Over
time, this leads to subtle but significant shifts: decisions feel less
owned, accountability becomes diffuse, and individuals may struggle to
articulate their role in AI-mediated processes.

This book exists to make those dynamics visible.

The purpose of *Human--AI Co-Agency* is to clarify what co-agency means
in practice, why it is essential for responsible and effective AI use,
and how it can be deliberately developed rather than assumed. Rather
than offering technical prescriptions or abstract ethical theory, the
book focuses on the lived realities of AI-supported work: how people
interpret outputs, how authority is exercised, how responsibility is
shared, and how judgement is sustained over time.

Co-agency, as used here, does not imply equality between humans and
machines, nor does it suggest that AI systems possess moral or
institutional agency. Instead, it refers to the **structured,
accountable interaction between human actors and AI systems within
decision-making processes**. It is about ensuring that humans remain
meaningfully in control---not by manually intervening at every step, but
by designing roles, boundaries, and escalation paths that preserve
agency where it matters most.

This focus distinguishes co-agency from more familiar framings such as
"human-in-the-loop" or "AI assistance." Those concepts often reduce
agency to a technical safeguard rather than a socio-organisational
capability. Co-agency, by contrast, recognises that agency is shaped by
culture, governance, incentives, and professional norms as much as by
system design.

Within the AI Capability Framework, co-agency is treated as a **core
domain** because it underpins all others. Ethical principles cannot be
enacted without clear agency. Governance mechanisms cannot function
without defined responsibility. Applied practice becomes risky or
incoherent when agency is ambiguous. Reflection and learning break down
when it is unclear who is accountable for outcomes.

This book therefore aims to provide readers with a robust, practical
understanding of co-agency as a capability that can be cultivated,
assessed, and renewed over time. By the end of the book, readers should
be able to recognise healthy and unhealthy patterns of co-agency in
their own contexts, articulate where agency currently sits, and identify
concrete steps for strengthening it.

## The Problem of Automation Without Agency

### Risks of delegation, abdication, and over-trust

The need for a co-agency perspective becomes clear when we examine a
recurring pattern in AI adoption: **automation without agency**.

Automation without agency occurs when AI systems are introduced into
decision-making processes without sufficient attention to how
responsibility, authority, and judgement are redistributed. In such
cases, work may become faster or more scalable, but it also becomes less
transparent and less accountable. Over time, this erodes both individual
confidence and institutional trust.

This problem rarely arises from malicious intent. More often, it emerges
from reasonable but incomplete assumptions. Organisations adopt AI to
manage complexity, reduce workload, or improve consistency. Individuals
use AI tools to support analysis, drafting, or pattern recognition. In
doing so, they may gradually rely on system outputs without revisiting
the question of who ultimately owns the decision.

Three related risks tend to characterise automation without agency:
**delegation**, **abdication**, and **over-trust**.

**Delegation** occurs when tasks or decisions are formally or informally
handed over to AI systems without clear criteria or limits. In some
cases, delegation is explicit: an AI system is authorised to make or
recommend decisions within a defined scope. In others, it is implicit:
repeated reliance on system outputs gradually shifts expectations about
who decides.

Delegation becomes problematic when it is not accompanied by clear
accountability. Individuals may follow AI recommendations because they
are efficient, consistent, or endorsed, even when those recommendations
conflict with contextual knowledge or professional judgement. Over time,
the act of deciding becomes conflated with the act of accepting system
output.

**Abdication** is a more subtle but equally concerning risk. It occurs
when humans retain nominal responsibility for decisions but lack the
practical means or confidence to exercise judgement. In these
situations, individuals may feel unable to challenge AI outputs, either
because they do not understand how they were produced, because
organisational norms discourage dissent, or because responsibility is
framed in ways that make challenge seem futile.

Abdication is often reinforced by organisational pressures. Performance
metrics, time constraints, or risk-averse cultures can make it easier to
defer to AI than to interrogate it. When errors occur, responsibility
may be retrospectively assigned to individuals who lacked genuine agency
at the time of decision-making.

**Over-trust** refers to misplaced confidence in AI systems'
reliability, objectivity, or appropriateness. This is not simply a
matter of misunderstanding technical limitations. Over-trust is shaped
by narratives about AI's authority, by the perceived legitimacy of
system outputs, and by the social dynamics of decision-making
environments.

When over-trust takes hold, AI outputs are treated as neutral or
definitive rather than as inputs requiring interpretation. This can lead
to reduced scrutiny, normalisation of error, and difficulty recognising
drift or bias. Over-trust is particularly dangerous in high-stakes or
sensitive contexts, where small errors can have significant
consequences.

Together, these risks create a situation in which AI appears to be
supporting decision-making while quietly undermining agency. Decisions
are made, but ownership is unclear. Responsibility exists, but it is
difficult to locate. Learning is hindered because it is unclear who
should reflect on outcomes or initiate change.

Automation without agency also has long-term organisational
consequences. As reliance on AI increases, individuals may lose
confidence in their own judgement or become reluctant to take
responsibility for outcomes. New staff may learn to defer rather than to
reason. Governance mechanisms may struggle to intervene because
practices have become normalised without documentation or review.

Crucially, these dynamics do not require fully autonomous systems to
emerge. They can arise in everyday uses of generative AI,
decision-support tools, or analytics platforms. The risk is not autonomy
itself, but **the gradual erosion of human agency through unexamined
reliance**.

This is why co-agency must be treated as a capability rather than a
safeguard. Technical controls alone cannot address delegation,
abdication, or over-trust. What is required is a deliberate approach to
designing roles, responsibilities, and decision processes that preserve
meaningful human involvement while allowing AI to contribute
productively.

The rest of this book explores how that can be achieved. It defines
co-agency in practical terms, examines how it develops over time,
identifies common failure modes, and shows how co-agency interacts with
ethics, governance, and applied practice. Throughout, the focus remains
on a single guiding question:

How can humans work with AI in ways that preserve agency,
accountability, and learning---especially as systems become more capable
and pervasive?

That question frames the chapters that follow and anchors co-agency as a
foundational element of sustainable AI capability.

# Position Within the AI Capability Framework

## Co-Agency as a Core Capability

### How shared agency underpins ethical and effective AI use

Within the AI Capability Framework, **Human--AI Co-Agency** occupies a
distinctive position. It is not simply one domain among others, nor is
it a specialised concern relevant only to certain contexts or roles.
Instead, co-agency functions as a *structural capability*---one that
shapes how all other domains are enacted in practice.

At its simplest, co-agency concerns how responsibility, authority, and
judgement are distributed when AI systems are involved in human work. At
a deeper level, it determines whether AI use strengthens or weakens
human decision-making over time. For this reason, co-agency underpins
ethical integrity, governance effectiveness, and the sustainability of
applied practice.

The AI Capability Framework treats co-agency as a core domain because
**AI systems do not act in isolation**. They operate within human
systems---organisations, institutions, professions, and social
arrangements that assign responsibility, define authority, and create
accountability. When AI is introduced into these systems, it inevitably
reshapes how decisions are made, how responsibility is perceived, and
how outcomes are justified.

Without explicit attention to co-agency, these shifts tend to occur
implicitly. Authority may migrate toward system outputs without
deliberate choice. Responsibility may become diffuse or ambiguous.
Judgement may be exercised less frequently or less confidently. Over
time, this can hollow out the very capabilities that AI was intended to
support.

Treating co-agency as a capability reframes these dynamics. It
recognises that **agency must be actively designed, supported, and
renewed**. Co-agency is not guaranteed by keeping a human "in the loop,"
nor by assigning nominal responsibility after the fact. It requires
clarity about decision boundaries, escalation paths, and interpretive
authority throughout AI-supported workflows.

From a capability perspective, ethical and effective AI use is
impossible without co-agency. Ethical principles depend on someone being
able to exercise judgement and take responsibility for consequences.
Governance mechanisms depend on knowing who decides, who reviews, and
who intervenes. Applied practice depends on individuals feeling
authorised to interpret, question, and adapt AI outputs rather than
simply execute them.

In this sense, co-agency acts as a *keystone domain*. When it is strong,
other capabilities can function coherently. When it is weak, even
well-designed ethical frameworks, governance structures, or technical
practices struggle to operate as intended.

Importantly, co-agency is not a fixed arrangement. It evolves as
technologies, organisational contexts, and forms of work change. Early
experimentation with AI often involves informal co-agency, where roles
and responsibilities are negotiated tacitly. As use becomes more
embedded, co-agency must be stabilised through clearer structures and
shared understanding. At more advanced stages, co-agency becomes
adaptive, allowing humans to recalibrate decision boundaries as systems
and contexts evolve.

By positioning co-agency as a core capability, the framework emphasises
that **agency is not something that can be "left to culture" or "assumed
to persist"**. It must be made visible, discussed explicitly, and
revisited over time. This book exists to support that work.

## 

## Relationships to Other Domains

### Links to ethics, governance, and applied practice

While co-agency is a distinct domain within the AI Capability Framework,
it does not operate independently. Its significance lies in how it
connects and gives effect to other domains---particularly ethics,
governance, and applied practice. Understanding these relationships is
essential for avoiding fragmented or superficial capability development.

### Co-Agency and Ethics, Equity, and Impact

Ethical AI use depends fundamentally on agency. Ethical principles such
as fairness, accountability, transparency, and responsibility are
meaningful only if there are humans who can interpret them, apply them,
and act on them in specific situations. Without co-agency, ethics risks
becoming abstract or symbolic rather than operational.

When co-agency is weak, ethical responsibility often becomes displaced.
Individuals may assume that ethical concerns are "handled by the
system," "covered by policy," or "someone else's job." In such
environments, harms may go unrecognised or unaddressed not because
people are indifferent, but because they lack the authority or clarity
needed to intervene.

Strong co-agency enables ethical capability by ensuring that:

- responsibility for ethical judgement is clearly located,

- individuals feel authorised to question AI-supported decisions,

- ethical concerns can be escalated and reviewed,

- and learning from ethical dilemmas informs future practice.

Equity and impact considerations are particularly sensitive to agency.
AI systems may differentially affect groups or individuals, often in
ways that are not immediately visible. Recognising and responding to
these effects requires human interpretation, contextual knowledge, and
moral judgement. Co-agency ensures that such interpretation remains part
of decision-making rather than being sidelined by efficiency or
automation.

In this way, co-agency does not replace ethical reasoning; it **enables
it to function in practice**.

### Co-Agency and Decision-Making and Governance

Governance structures are only as effective as the agency they support.
Policies, oversight bodies, and accountability mechanisms rely on clear
understandings of who decides, who reviews, and who is responsible for
outcomes. Co-agency provides the connective tissue that allows
governance to operate meaningfully.

When co-agency is underdeveloped, governance often becomes procedural
rather than purposeful. Rules may exist, but they fail to influence
everyday practice. Approval processes may be bypassed or treated as
formalities. Accountability may be retrospective and punitive rather
than constructive.

By contrast, strong co-agency supports governance by:

- clarifying decision boundaries and escalation routes,

- enabling individuals to act within governance frameworks confidently,

- making responsibility visible at appropriate levels,

- and supporting timely intervention when practices drift.

Co-agency also helps governance adapt. As AI use evolves, rigid
governance structures can become misaligned with practice. Co-agency
allows organisations to recognise these misalignments early and adjust
governance in response, rather than relying solely on compliance or
enforcement.

In this sense, co-agency and governance are mutually reinforcing.
Governance provides structure and legitimacy; co-agency ensures that
structure is inhabited and enacted by human judgement.

### Co-Agency and Applied Practice and Innovation

Applied practice is where AI capability becomes visible. It is also
where co-agency is most directly tested. Day-to-day use of AI systems
involves countless micro-decisions: how to interpret outputs, when to
rely on them, when to override them, and when to stop using them
altogether.

Without co-agency, applied practice tends to drift toward automation by
default. AI outputs become accepted as "the answer," and human
involvement becomes limited to execution or oversight without genuine
authority. This may appear efficient in the short term, but it
undermines learning, accountability, and adaptability.

Strong co-agency supports applied practice by:

- legitimising human interpretation and challenge,

- enabling experimentation within clear boundaries,

- supporting documentation and reflection,

- and ensuring that innovation remains aligned with organisational
  purpose and values.

Innovation, in the AI Capability Framework, is not equated with novelty
or speed. It is understood as the ability to explore and adapt
responsibly. Co-agency is what makes such exploration safe and
sustainable. It allows organisations to learn from experimentation
without normalising risk or eroding trust.

Applied practice also feeds back into co-agency. As individuals gain
experience working with AI systems, their understanding of appropriate
roles and boundaries deepens. Reflection on practice informs refinements
to co-agency arrangements, creating a virtuous cycle of learning and
adaptation.

### Co-Agency as a Connector Domain

Taken together, these relationships highlight why co-agency cannot be
treated as a specialist concern or a secondary consideration. It
connects ethical intent to practical action, governance structures to
lived decision-making, and innovation to accountability.

Within the AI Capability Framework, co-agency functions as a **connector
domain**. It translates abstract principles into concrete behaviour. It
ensures that capability development remains human-centred even as
systems become more capable. It provides the conditions under which
other domains can operate coherently rather than in isolation.

This book builds on that positioning. The chapters that follow define
co-agency more precisely, explore how it develops over time, identify
common failure modes, and show how it can be strengthened across
different contexts. Throughout, the focus remains on enabling humans to
retain meaningful agency in AI-supported work---not by resisting
technology, but by shaping how it is integrated into human systems.

# Defining Human--AI Co-Agency

## What Co-Agency Is

### Shared responsibility, decision boundaries, and role clarity

Human--AI co-agency refers to the **structured and accountable way in
which humans and AI systems jointly participate in work,
decision-making, and sense-making**, while responsibility and authority
remain clearly human. It is not a claim about what AI *is*, but a
description of how AI is *situated within human systems*.

At its core, co-agency is about **how decisions happen** when AI systems
are involved: who initiates action, who interprets outputs, who decides
whether to act, and who is accountable for consequences. These questions
are often left implicit in AI adoption, but they determine whether AI
use strengthens or weakens human capability over time.

Co-agency recognises that AI systems increasingly shape human activity
without acting independently. They generate options, surface patterns,
draft content, rank alternatives, and influence attention. These
contributions can be powerful, but they do not constitute agency in the
human sense. AI systems do not hold values, bear responsibility, or
participate in institutional accountability. Co-agency therefore does
not mean shared moral or legal agency; it means **designed interaction**
within human responsibility structures.

Three elements are central to co-agency as a capability: **shared
responsibility**, **decision boundaries**, and **role clarity**.

### Shared responsibility

Shared responsibility does not mean diluted responsibility. In
co-agency, responsibility remains human, but it is often **distributed
across roles and levels**. Individuals, teams, and institutions may each
carry responsibility for different aspects of AI-supported work:
interpretation, approval, oversight, escalation, or learning.

Co-agency requires that these responsibilities are:

- explicit rather than assumed,

- understood by those who hold them,

- and supported by appropriate authority and resources.

When responsibility is unclear, individuals may feel exposed or
constrained. They may follow AI outputs defensively to avoid blame, or
disengage from judgement altogether. Conversely, when responsibility is
well-designed, individuals are more likely to exercise judgement
confidently and transparently.

Shared responsibility also includes responsibility for **learning**.
Co-agency is not only about who decides, but about who reflects, who
revises practice, and who initiates change when AI use produces
unexpected outcomes. Capability depends on responsibility extending
beyond execution to include interpretation and renewal.

### Decision boundaries

Decision boundaries define **where AI contribution ends and human
judgement begins**, and where that judgement may be escalated or
reviewed. These boundaries are rarely fixed; they vary by context, risk,
and maturity. What matters is not rigid specification, but clarity and
adaptability.

In capable co-agency arrangements:

- AI outputs are treated as inputs to decision-making, not as decisions
  themselves.

- Humans understand when reliance on AI is appropriate and when it is
  not.

- There are recognised points at which judgement must be exercised,
  questioned, or escalated.

Decision boundaries help prevent both over-automation and under-use.
They legitimise challenge and override without framing them as failure.
They also support consistency by reducing reliance on individual
discretion alone.

Crucially, decision boundaries are not only technical. They are
organisational and cultural. Even when systems are designed to allow
human intervention, social norms or performance pressures may discourage
it. Co-agency therefore requires boundaries to be reinforced through
leadership signals, governance structures, and professional norms.

### Role clarity

Role clarity concerns how human roles are defined and understood in
AI-supported systems. This includes not only formal roles, such as
decision-makers or reviewers, but also informal roles related to
interpretation, translation, and sense-making.

In many AI deployments, roles are blurred. Individuals may be expected
to "use AI responsibly" without guidance on what that responsibility
entails. Others may be assigned nominal accountability without access to
the information or authority needed to fulfil it. Co-agency addresses
this by making roles explicit and aligning them with actual practice.

Role clarity supports:

- confidence in decision-making,

- accountability that feels legitimate rather than imposed,

- and coordination across teams and functions.

It also supports learning. When roles are clear, it is easier to
identify where breakdowns occurred and where improvements are needed.
When roles are ambiguous, learning is often lost in blame or
defensiveness.

Taken together, shared responsibility, decision boundaries, and role
clarity define co-agency as a **designed capability** rather than an
emergent by-product of AI use. They enable humans to remain meaningfully
involved in AI-supported work without reverting to manual control or
resisting augmentation.

Importantly, co-agency does not imply symmetry. Humans and AI systems
contribute in different ways, with different forms of authority.
Co-agency is not about balancing power, but about **preserving human
agency within augmented systems**.

## 

## What Co-Agency Is Not

### Why co-agency is not autonomy, replacement, or partnership rhetoric

Clarifying what co-agency is *not* is essential, because many popular
narratives about AI obscure or distort the concept. These narratives
often arise from technical, commercial, or rhetorical framings that
emphasise capability without responsibility.

### Co-agency is not autonomy

Co-agency should not be confused with system autonomy. Autonomous AI
systems are often defined by their ability to act without human
intervention. Co-agency, by contrast, is defined by **intentional human
involvement**.

Autonomy narratives tend to focus on efficiency and scale, framing human
intervention as friction. From a capability perspective, this framing is
misleading. Human judgement is not a bottleneck to be eliminated; it is
a capability to be preserved.

Even when AI systems operate automatically within defined parameters,
co-agency requires that humans:

- set those parameters intentionally,

- monitor outcomes,

- and retain authority to intervene, revise, or withdraw use.

Autonomy without agency creates systems that are difficult to govern,
difficult to learn from, and difficult to justify ethically. Co-agency
insists that meaningful human responsibility remains intact, even as
systems become more capable.

### Co-agency is not replacement

Co-agency also differs fundamentally from replacement narratives. Claims
that AI will replace human roles often focus on task substitution rather
than on the broader functions those roles serve. While AI may perform
certain tasks more efficiently, it does not replace the **institutional
and ethical functions of human roles**.

Replacement framings obscure the ways in which AI reshapes work rather
than eliminates it. They encourage abdication by framing human
involvement as temporary or obsolete. In practice, this often leads to
hidden labour: humans continue to interpret, correct, and contextualise
AI outputs without recognition or authority.

Co-agency rejects replacement as a goal. Instead, it asks how human
roles must evolve in response to AI, and how responsibility should be
redistributed rather than erased. It recognises that many human
contributions---such as judgement under uncertainty, ethical reasoning,
and accountability---cannot be automated without undermining legitimacy
and trust.

### Co-agency is not partnership rhetoric

Finally, co-agency should not be confused with rhetorical claims about
"AI as a partner" or "collaboration between humans and machines." While
such language may be intended to humanise technology, it often creates
conceptual confusion.

Partnership implies shared agency, mutual responsibility, and reciprocal
obligation. AI systems do not possess these qualities. Treating them as
partners risks obscuring where responsibility truly lies and may
encourage misplaced trust or emotional attachment.

Co-agency does not anthropomorphise AI. It treats AI systems as powerful
tools and contributors within human systems, not as social actors. This
distinction is critical for maintaining accountability and avoiding
moral confusion.

By rejecting autonomy, replacement, and partnership rhetoric, co-agency
maintains a clear boundary between **capability and responsibility**. It
allows organisations to take advantage of AI's strengths without
surrendering control or obscuring accountability.

### Co-Agency as a practical framing

What distinguishes co-agency from these alternatives is its **practical
orientation**. It is not a vision of the future, but a framework for
present-day decision-making. It asks concrete questions:

- Who decides?

- Who is responsible?

- Where are boundaries drawn?

- How is learning supported?

These questions can be answered differently across contexts, but they
cannot be ignored. Co-agency provides a way to answer them intentionally
rather than by default.

In doing so, it shifts AI adoption from a technical exercise to a
capability-building process. It acknowledges that AI will continue to
change, but insists that human agency must remain visible, accountable,
and renewable.

The next sections of this book build on this definition by examining how
co-agency is enacted in practice, how it develops over time, and how
common failure modes can be identified and addressed. Together, they
show how co-agency can move from an abstract concern to a lived
organisational capability.

# Co-Agency in Practice

## Human Roles in AI-Supported Work

### Judgement, interpretation, accountability

Human--AI co-agency becomes real not through policy statements or system
architecture, but through **human roles enacted in practice**.
Regardless of how advanced AI systems become, it is human judgement,
interpretation, and accountability that determine whether AI use
strengthens or undermines capability.

In AI-supported work, humans do not simply "use" AI. They **frame
problems, interpret outputs, decide whether to act, and take
responsibility for consequences**. These roles are often compressed into
a single moment of interaction, but analytically they are distinct.
Co-agency depends on keeping these functions visible and supported
rather than allowing them to collapse into unexamined acceptance of
system output.

### Judgement

Judgement is the capacity to decide *whether* and *how* to act in a
specific context, given incomplete information and competing values. AI
systems may generate options, rankings, or recommendations, but they do
not exercise judgement in this sense. They do not understand stakes,
legitimacy, or institutional responsibility.

In practice, judgement involves:

- assessing the relevance of AI output to the current context,

- recognising when outputs conflict with contextual knowledge or ethical
  considerations,

- deciding when to rely on AI and when to override or ignore it.

Judgement is particularly critical in situations of uncertainty,
novelty, or high stakes. These are precisely the situations in which AI
outputs may appear persuasive while being least reliable. Co-agency
requires that individuals are not only permitted but **expected** to
exercise judgement, even when this slows down decision-making or
introduces friction.

When judgement is unsupported, individuals may default to system outputs
to avoid risk or responsibility. Over time, this erodes professional
confidence and weakens organisational learning. Strong co-agency treats
judgement as a capability to be cultivated, not a liability to be
minimised.

### Interpretation

Interpretation sits between system output and decision. AI systems
produce outputs that require meaning-making: probabilities, suggestions,
drafts, summaries, or classifications. These outputs do not speak for
themselves. Their significance depends on context, intent, and
consequence.

Human interpretation involves:

- understanding what an AI output represents and what it does not,

- situating outputs within broader evidence or experience,

- recognising uncertainty, bias, or limitation.

Interpretation is often invisible labour. Individuals may be expected to
"sense-check" or "apply judgement" without formal recognition or
support. In weak co-agency arrangements, interpretation is treated as an
informal skill rather than a defined role, making it difficult to
resource, train, or evaluate.

Co-agency strengthens interpretation by:

- acknowledging it as a core human contribution,

- clarifying when interpretation is required,

- and ensuring that interpreters have access to relevant context and
  authority.

This is particularly important in collaborative environments, where
interpretations must be shared, discussed, and sometimes contested.
Co-agency legitimises disagreement and deliberation rather than treating
divergence from AI output as inefficiency or error.

### Accountability

Accountability anchors co-agency in institutional reality. It concerns
who is answerable for decisions and outcomes, both internally and
externally. While AI systems may influence decisions, they cannot be
accountable in organisational, legal, or ethical terms.

In AI-supported work, accountability often becomes blurred. Individuals
may feel responsible for outcomes without having exercised real
authority, or they may defer to AI outputs while retaining nominal
accountability. Both situations are problematic.

Effective co-agency aligns accountability with:

- decision authority,

- access to information,

- and the ability to intervene or escalate.

This alignment ensures that responsibility is neither unfairly imposed
nor quietly displaced. It also supports learning. When accountability is
clear, organisations can examine outcomes constructively rather than
defensively, identifying where judgement, interpretation, or system
design needs adjustment.

Accountability in co-agency is not solely about blame or liability. It
is also about **ownership of learning**. Who is responsible for
reviewing outcomes? Who initiates change when practices drift? Who
ensures that lessons are integrated into future work? These questions
are central to sustaining capability over time.

Taken together, judgement, interpretation, and accountability define the
human side of co-agency. They are not optional add-ons to AI use, but
the conditions under which AI can contribute productively to human
systems.

## AI Roles in Human Systems

### Augmentation, pattern detection, and support functions

Understanding co-agency also requires clarity about what AI systems *do*
within human systems---and, equally importantly, what they do not do.
Co-agency does not assign agency to AI; it assigns **functions** that
support human work.

In practice, AI systems contribute in three broad ways:
**augmentation**, **pattern detection**, and **support functions**.
These roles are descriptive rather than prescriptive; they help clarify
how AI can be integrated without obscuring responsibility.

### Augmentation

Augmentation refers to AI's ability to extend human capacity by reducing
cognitive load, accelerating routine tasks, or enabling exploration at
scale. In this role, AI supports humans in doing work they already do,
more efficiently or more expansively.

Examples of augmentation include:

- drafting or summarising content for human review,

- generating alternative options or perspectives,

- supporting scenario exploration or brainstorming.

In co-agency terms, augmentation is effective when it:

- leaves decision authority clearly with humans,

- invites interpretation rather than compliance,

- and supports learning rather than substitution.

Problems arise when augmentation quietly becomes delegation. When
AI-generated drafts or recommendations are accepted by default,
augmentation shifts toward automation. Co-agency requires that
augmentation remains explicitly framed as support, not replacement.

### Pattern detection

AI systems excel at identifying patterns across large datasets or
complex inputs. This capability can be valuable in highlighting trends,
anomalies, or correlations that humans may struggle to detect unaided.

In human systems, pattern detection supports:

- situational awareness,

- risk identification,

- and exploratory analysis.

However, patterns are not explanations, and correlations are not
judgements. AI systems surface signals; humans must decide what those
signals mean and how to respond. Co-agency depends on maintaining this
distinction.

Pattern detection becomes risky when:

- outputs are treated as conclusions rather than prompts,

- uncertainty is ignored,

- or contextual factors are excluded.

Effective co-agency ensures that pattern detection feeds into human
sense-making processes rather than bypassing them. It also requires
awareness of how training data, assumptions, and system design shape
what patterns are visible and which are obscured.

### Support functions

Beyond augmentation and pattern detection, AI systems increasingly
perform support functions that structure human work. These may include
organising information, prioritising tasks, or mediating access to
resources.

Support functions influence behaviour indirectly. By shaping attention,
workflow, or perceived relevance, AI systems can steer decisions without
making them explicitly. This makes support functions particularly
significant from a co-agency perspective.

Co-agency requires that:

- the influence of support functions is recognised,

- humans retain the ability to override or reconfigure them,

- and their effects are subject to reflection and review.

When support functions operate invisibly, they can subtly erode agency
by narrowing choice or normalising certain behaviours. Making these
functions visible and contestable is essential for maintaining
meaningful human involvement.

### AI roles as designed contributions

Across these roles, a key principle of co-agency is that **AI
contributions are designed, not discovered**. How AI augments, detects
patterns, or supports work depends on choices made by humans: choices
about scope, boundaries, and integration.

Treating AI roles as designed contributions reinforces accountability.
It reminds organisations that AI behaviour is not inevitable, and that
human decisions shape how systems function in practice. This perspective
also supports adaptation. As needs and contexts change, AI roles can be
reconfigured without abandoning co-agency.

Importantly, recognising AI roles does not require detailed technical
understanding. What matters is functional clarity: knowing what AI is
contributing, how that contribution influences decisions, and where
human judgement remains essential.

## Co-Agency as a practical arrangement

Co-agency in practice emerges from the **alignment of human roles and AI
roles**. When judgement, interpretation, and accountability are clearly
supported, and AI functions are well understood, co-agency becomes a
lived capability rather than an abstract ideal.

Breakdowns in co-agency often occur at the seams: where interpretation
is assumed but unsupported, where accountability exists without
authority, or where AI support quietly becomes directive. Recognising
these seams is the first step toward strengthening capability.

The following chapters examine how co-agency develops over time, how
risks and failure modes emerge, and how co-agency can be integrated with
ethics and governance. Together, they show how co-agency can move from
an implicit assumption to an intentional, renewable capability embedded
in everyday practice.

# Developing Co-Agency Over Time

Human--AI co-agency is not established at the moment AI is introduced,
nor is it achieved through a single design decision. It develops over
time, shaped by experience, reflection, and organisational learning. As
AI use evolves, so too must the ways in which responsibility, judgement,
and authority are exercised.

This chapter describes three **developmental patterns** commonly
observed as co-agency matures: early co-agency, developing co-agency,
and advanced co-agency. These are not stages to be completed in
sequence, nor benchmarks to be achieved uniformly. In practice,
organisations may exhibit different patterns simultaneously across
teams, functions, or contexts.

The purpose of this framing is not classification, but **recognition**.
Understanding how co-agency typically develops helps organisations
anticipate challenges, avoid common pitfalls, and support capability
growth deliberately rather than reactively.

## Early Co-Agency

### Exploration, uncertainty, and experimentation

Early co-agency emerges when AI systems are first introduced into a
context. At this stage, use is often exploratory, informal, and unevenly
distributed. Individuals and teams experiment with AI to understand what
it can do, where it might be useful, and how it fits into existing work.

This phase is characterised by **uncertainty**. Roles and
responsibilities are rarely explicit. Decision boundaries are fluid or
unarticulated. Individuals rely on personal judgement and informal norms
to decide when and how to use AI. This uncertainty is not inherently
problematic; in fact, it is often necessary for learning.

In early co-agency, experimentation plays a valuable role. It allows
people to test assumptions, surface limitations, and discover
opportunities that were not apparent in abstract planning. It also helps
organisations identify where AI genuinely adds value and where it
creates friction or risk.

However, early co-agency is also fragile. Because roles and boundaries
are unclear, responsibility may be unevenly distributed. Some
individuals may take on disproportionate risk, while others remain
disengaged. Judgement may be exercised inconsistently, and learning may
remain local rather than shared.

Common characteristics of early co-agency include:

- reliance on individual initiative rather than collective practice,

- informal interpretation of AI outputs,

- limited documentation or review,

- and uncertainty about accountability when outcomes are contested.

At this stage, failures or missteps are often treated as personal rather
than systemic. This can discourage transparency and learning if
individuals feel exposed or unsupported.

Capability-oriented organisations recognise early co-agency as a
**learning phase**, not a compliance problem. They focus on creating
psychological safety, encouraging reflection, and capturing insight
rather than enforcing premature standardisation. At the same time, they
remain attentive to risks, particularly where experimentation intersects
with high-stakes decisions.

The transition out of early co-agency begins when organisations
recognise recurring patterns in use and outcome, and when the need for
clearer roles and boundaries becomes apparent.

## Developing Co-Agency

### Stabilising roles and decision boundaries

Developing co-agency emerges as AI use becomes more routine and
embedded. At this stage, organisations begin to formalise aspects of
co-agency in response to experience. Roles are clarified, decision
boundaries are articulated, and expectations become more consistent
across contexts.

This phase is marked by **stabilisation**. Rather than relying solely on
individual discretion, organisations establish shared understandings of
when AI may be used, how outputs should be interpreted, and where
responsibility lies. Governance mechanisms begin to align more closely
with practice.

Key features of developing co-agency include:

- clearer articulation of decision authority and accountability,

- explicit recognition of interpretation as a role,

- documented practices and guidance informed by experience,

- and emerging escalation pathways for uncertainty or disagreement.

Stabilisation does not mean rigidity. Effective developing co-agency
preserves space for judgement and adaptation while reducing ambiguity
that undermines confidence or consistency. The goal is not to eliminate
discretion, but to **support it**.

At this stage, organisations often confront tensions between flexibility
and control. Too much formalisation can stifle learning or innovation;
too little can leave individuals exposed or practices incoherent.
Co-agency development involves negotiating these tensions rather than
resolving them once and for all.

Learning becomes more collective during this phase. Reflection moves
beyond individual experience to include team-level or organisational
review. Outcomes are discussed, not just executed. Misalignments between
AI output and human judgement are examined for insight rather than
blame.

Developing co-agency also requires attention to **role alignment**.
Accountability must be matched with authority. Individuals expected to
exercise judgement must have access to information, time, and support.
Without this alignment, formal responsibility may exist without
practical agency.

This phase is often where co-agency either strengthens or stalls. If
stabilisation becomes overly prescriptive, co-agency may harden into
procedural compliance. If stabilisation is avoided altogether,
early-phase fragility persists. Capability-oriented organisations treat
developing co-agency as an ongoing adjustment rather than a one-time
design task.

## 

## Advanced Co-Agency

### Adaptive use, reflective escalation, and learning

Advanced co-agency is characterised by **adaptability and reflection**.
At this level, organisations are able to adjust co-agency arrangements
in response to changing technologies, contexts, and insights. Agency is
not only stabilised; it is **actively maintained**.

In advanced co-agency, roles and decision boundaries are well
understood, but not fixed. Individuals know when and how to exercise
judgement, and they are supported in escalating uncertainty or challenge
without stigma. Escalation is treated as a normal part of responsible
practice rather than a failure.

Key features of advanced co-agency include:

- confidence in exercising and challenging judgement,

- explicit mechanisms for reviewing and revising AI use,

- integration of learning into governance and practice,

- and alignment between organisational values and everyday decisions.

Advanced co-agency supports **reflective escalation**. When AI-supported
decisions raise concern, individuals know where to take those concerns
and what will happen next. Review processes are timely, constructive,
and oriented toward improvement rather than punishment.

Learning at this stage is institutional rather than incidental. Insights
from AI use inform training, governance updates, and strategic
decisions. Reflection feeds back into orientation, ensuring that
assumptions evolve alongside experience.

Importantly, advanced co-agency does not imply maximal automation or
maximal control. It implies **appropriate agency** for context and risk.
In some situations, AI may operate with minimal human intervention
within well-defined bounds. In others, close human involvement may be
required. Advanced co-agency allows organisations to make these
distinctions deliberately rather than by default.

Advanced co-agency is also resilient. When technologies change or
failures occur, organisations are able to respond without losing
coherence or trust. Responsibility remains clear, learning is
prioritised, and adaptation is purposeful.

## 

## Co-Agency development as continuous practice

Across these patterns, one principle remains constant: **co-agency must
be renewed**. Even advanced arrangements can degrade if they are not
revisited. New systems, staff turnover, external pressures, or shifting
incentives can all erode agency over time.

Developing co-agency therefore involves ongoing attention to:

- how roles are understood and enacted,

- whether decision boundaries remain appropriate,

- and how learning is captured and acted upon.

By framing co-agency as a developmental capability rather than a fixed
state, organisations are better equipped to navigate change without
surrendering agency or accountability.

The chapters that follow examine risks and failure modes that can
undermine co-agency at any stage, and explore how co-agency integrates
with ethics and governance to support sustainable AI capability.

# Risks and Failure Modes

Human--AI co-agency does not fail all at once. It erodes gradually,
often through well-intentioned practices that go unexamined as AI use
becomes normalised. The risks described in this chapter are not edge
cases or rare pathologies; they are **predictable failure modes** that
arise when co-agency is under-specified, unsupported, or taken for
granted.

Two failure modes are particularly persistent across sectors and
contexts: **automation bias and over-reliance**, and **responsibility
drift and ambiguity**. They frequently co-occur and reinforce one
another, creating systems that appear functional on the surface while
quietly undermining judgement, accountability, and learning.

Understanding these risks is essential for maintaining healthy co-agency
over time.

## Automation Bias and Over-Reliance

Automation bias refers to the tendency for humans to favour, defer to,
or unquestioningly accept outputs generated by automated systems, even
when those outputs conflict with contextual knowledge, professional
judgement, or other evidence. In AI-supported work, automation bias is
rarely the result of naïveté. It is more often a product of **social,
organisational, and cognitive pressures**.

AI systems often present outputs with a sense of authority: ranked
lists, confident language, apparent consistency, or quantitative
precision. Over time, repeated exposure to such outputs can recalibrate
human expectations. What begins as assistance gradually becomes default.

Over-reliance develops when:

- AI outputs are consistently treated as "good enough,"

- human challenge is framed as inefficiency or resistance,

- or questioning AI is perceived as risky, time-consuming, or futile.

In these environments, judgement is not explicitly removed, but it is
**discouraged by context**.

Automation bias is reinforced by several common conditions:

- **Time pressure**: When speed is prioritised, AI outputs offer a
  convenient shortcut.

- **Performance metrics**: When efficiency or throughput is measured
  without attention to judgement quality, reliance is rewarded.

- **Organisational signalling**: When leadership endorses AI use without
  equal emphasis on challenge and review, deference becomes normative.

- **Opacity**: When system behaviour is difficult to understand or
  interrogate, humans may defer rather than risk being wrong.

The consequences of automation bias are cumulative. Early reliance may
appear harmless, but over time it reshapes behaviour. Individuals stop
checking outputs as carefully. Anomalies are rationalised rather than
investigated. The ability to recognise error atrophies.

Crucially, automation bias does not require fully autonomous systems. It
arises readily in decision-support, generative, or advisory
systems---particularly when outputs are framed as recommendations rather
than suggestions.

From a co-agency perspective, automation bias represents a **failure of
judgement support**, not a failure of human competence. Individuals may
retain nominal authority, but lack the conditions needed to exercise it
meaningfully.

Healthy co-agency counteracts automation bias by:

- legitimising scepticism and challenge,

- reinforcing the interpretive role of humans,

- and making reliance a conscious choice rather than an unexamined
  habit.

Where these conditions are absent, over-reliance becomes
self-reinforcing. The more humans defer, the less capable they feel of
intervening. The less they intervene, the more authoritative the system
appears.

## 

## Responsibility Drift and Ambiguity

Responsibility drift refers to the gradual misalignment between **who
appears responsible** and **who is actually able to act** in
AI-supported systems. Unlike automation bias, which centres on decision
acceptance, responsibility drift concerns **ownership and accountability
over time**.

In early AI adoption, responsibility is often clear because use is
limited and visible. As systems become embedded, responsibility can
become diffuse. Multiple actors contribute to outcomes, but no single
actor feels authorised or obligated to take ownership when things go
wrong.

Responsibility drift commonly emerges through:

- layered workflows where AI influences decisions indirectly,

- shared or collective decision-making without clear escalation paths,

- or governance structures that assign accountability without authority.

In such contexts, individuals may be held responsible in principle but
lack practical agency. They may be expected to "own" decisions shaped by
AI without having had meaningful opportunity to question or alter them.

Responsibility ambiguity is particularly corrosive to learning. When
outcomes are contested or harmful, organisations struggle to identify
where intervention is needed. Reviews focus on surface errors rather
than structural causes. Blame may be assigned retrospectively, but
little changes in practice.

Responsibility drift also interacts with automation bias. As humans
defer to AI outputs, responsibility subtly shifts toward the system---at
least in perception. Yet when scrutiny arises, responsibility snaps back
to individuals or teams who may not have exercised genuine agency at the
time.

This dynamic creates **moral and organisational tension**. Individuals
feel exposed. Trust erodes. Defensive practices emerge, such as rigid
adherence to AI outputs or excessive documentation designed to deflect
blame rather than support learning.

From a capability perspective, responsibility drift signals a breakdown
in co-agency design. Responsibility exists, but it is not supported by:

- clear decision authority,

- access to relevant information,

- or recognised escalation mechanisms.

Healthy co-agency requires responsibility to be **matched with agency**.
Individuals must not only be accountable, but also empowered to act,
challenge, and revise practice. Where this alignment is missing,
responsibility becomes performative rather than functional.

## 

## How failure modes compound

Automation bias and responsibility drift rarely operate in isolation.
Together, they create systems that appear efficient but are brittle
under stress.

- Automation bias reduces human intervention.

- Reduced intervention weakens clarity about who is responsible.

- Weak responsibility discourages challenge.

- Lack of challenge reinforces reliance.

This feedback loop is difficult to detect from within, particularly when
systems continue to produce acceptable outcomes most of the time.
Failure modes often become visible only when stakes rise, contexts
change, or errors accumulate.

Importantly, these risks are not resolved by technical fixes alone.
Adding explanations, dashboards, or audit trails may help, but they do
not address the underlying **capability gap**. What is needed is
deliberate attention to how humans are expected and supported to
exercise agency.

## 

## Recognising early warning signs

Capability-oriented organisations monitor for signals that co-agency is
eroding. These signals are often behavioural rather than technical.

Common early indicators include:

- declining discussion or disagreement around AI outputs,

- increasing discomfort with overriding or questioning systems,

- unclear answers to questions about accountability,

- reliance on "the system decided" as an explanation,

- and difficulty identifying who should initiate review or change.

These signals are not evidence of failure, but opportunities for
intervention. When recognised early, they can prompt reflection,
clarification, and adjustment before risks become entrenched.

## 

## Reframing risk as capability maintenance

The purpose of this chapter is not to discourage AI use or to frame AI
as inherently risky. Rather, it is to highlight that **risk emerges when
co-agency is neglected**.

Automation bias and responsibility drift are symptoms of under-developed
capability, not of bad actors or flawed intentions. Addressing them
requires strengthening judgement support, clarifying responsibility, and
embedding learning into practice.

The chapters that follow turn from diagnosis to integration. They
explore how co-agency interacts with ethics and governance, and how it
can be reinforced through decision structures rather than undermined by
them. In doing so, they show how co-agency can remain robust even as AI
systems become more capable and pervasive.

# Integrating Co-Agency With Other Capabilities

Human--AI co-agency does not operate in isolation. While it is a
distinct capability domain, it is also a **structural hinge** that
connects ethical intent, governance design, and everyday practice. When
co-agency is weak or under-specified, other capabilities may exist in
name but fail in execution. When co-agency is strong, ethical
commitments become actionable and governance structures become enabling
rather than obstructive.

This chapter focuses on two critical integrations: co-agency with
**ethics and accountability**, and co-agency with **governance and
decision structures**. Together, these relationships determine whether
AI-supported systems remain trustworthy, adaptable, and human-centred
over time.

## Ethics and Accountability

Ethical principles are often articulated at a high level: fairness,
transparency, responsibility, harm reduction, respect for persons. These
principles are widely endorsed, yet organisations consistently struggle
to enact them in practice. One reason for this gap is that ethics is
frequently treated as a set of constraints or values rather than as a
**capability that depends on agency**.

Co-agency is the mechanism through which ethics becomes operational.

Ethical accountability requires that someone can:

- recognise when a value is at stake,

- interpret how that value applies in context,

- and act on that interpretation in a timely and legitimate way.

Without clear co-agency, ethical responsibility becomes diffuse.
Individuals may recognise ethical concerns but feel unable to intervene.
Teams may assume that responsibility sits elsewhere. Governance bodies
may articulate values without visibility into how decisions are actually
made.

Co-agency addresses this gap by clarifying who is expected to notice,
who is authorised to decide, and who is accountable for outcomes in
AI-supported work.

### Ethics as situated judgement

Ethical issues in AI rarely present themselves as obvious rule
violations. More often, they appear as trade-offs, tensions, or
uncertainties: efficiency versus fairness, consistency versus contextual
sensitivity, innovation versus risk. These situations cannot be resolved
by principles alone. They require judgement.

Co-agency provides the conditions under which ethical judgement can be
exercised:

- humans retain interpretive authority,

- AI outputs are treated as inputs rather than verdicts,

- and escalation pathways exist when ethical uncertainty arises.

Where co-agency is weak, ethical reasoning is displaced. Individuals may
defer to AI outputs even when they conflict with values, or rely on
policy language to avoid making difficult judgements. Ethics becomes
retrospective---invoked after harm occurs---rather than prospective and
embedded in practice.

### Accountability without agency is performative

Many organisations assign ethical accountability without ensuring
corresponding agency. Individuals are told they are "responsible" for
AI-influenced decisions, yet lack meaningful control over system design,
output interpretation, or escalation processes.

This mismatch creates performative accountability. Responsibility exists
on paper, but not in practice. When issues arise, individuals may be
blamed for outcomes they could not realistically influence.

Healthy co-agency aligns accountability with authority. It ensures that
those who are expected to uphold ethical standards:

- have access to relevant information,

- are empowered to question or override AI outputs,

- and are supported when ethical concerns require delay, review, or
  refusal.

This alignment is essential not only for fairness, but for
sustainability. Systems that repeatedly place individuals in untenable
positions erode trust and invite defensive behaviour.

### Ethics as an ongoing capability

Ethics is not resolved at design time. Values must be interpreted
repeatedly as contexts change, uses expand, and impacts become visible.
Co-agency supports this ongoing ethical capability by embedding
reflection and learning into decision-making processes.

When co-agency is integrated with ethics:

- ethical concerns are surfaced earlier,

- responsibility is shared rather than displaced,

- and learning feeds back into future practice.

In this sense, co-agency does not constrain ethical ambition; it enables
it. Without co-agency, ethics remains aspirational. With co-agency,
ethics becomes lived practice.

## Governance and Decision Structures

Governance is often misunderstood as a set of controls imposed on
practice. In reality, governance shapes how agency is distributed,
constrained, and supported. Governance that ignores co-agency either
centralises authority excessively or disperses responsibility so widely
that no one can act.

Co-agency and governance are therefore inseparable.

Governance answers questions such as:

- Who is authorised to decide?

- Under what conditions?

- With what oversight?

- And how are decisions reviewed, challenged, or revised?

Co-agency determines whether those answers are meaningful in practice.

### Governance as an enabler of agency

Effective governance does not remove judgement; it **structures it**. It
clarifies boundaries so that individuals know when they can act
autonomously, when consultation is required, and when escalation is
expected.

When governance is aligned with co-agency:

- decision rights are explicit,

- accountability pathways are visible,

- and uncertainty is treated as a signal for review rather than failure.

In contrast, governance that is detached from co-agency often produces
one of two outcomes:

- excessive rigidity, where individuals defer all decisions upward and
  innovation stalls;

- or informal workarounds, where governance is bypassed to "get things
  done."

Both outcomes undermine capability.

### Decision structures and escalation

AI-supported decisions often sit at the intersection of multiple
domains: technical feasibility, ethical acceptability, operational
impact, and reputational risk. No single individual or role can
reasonably hold all relevant expertise.

Co-agency requires decision structures that support **collective
judgement without diffusing responsibility**. This includes:

- clear escalation routes when AI outputs are contested,

- mechanisms for pausing or revisiting decisions,

- and defined roles for review and oversight.

Without these structures, individuals may feel compelled to accept AI
outputs even when uncertain, simply because no alternative pathway
exists.

Escalation should not be framed as failure. In a capability-oriented
system, escalation is a sign that co-agency is functioning: uncertainty
is recognised, responsibility is taken seriously, and decisions are
revisited rather than rushed.

### Governance across the lifecycle

Governance is not static. As AI use matures, decision structures must
adapt. Early experimentation may require lighter governance and closer
human oversight. As practices stabilise, governance can shift toward
consistency and accountability. When contexts change, governance must be
revisited.

Co-agency provides continuity across these phases. It ensures that
governance evolves in response to lived practice rather than being
frozen at an early moment of uncertainty or enthusiasm.

Capability-oriented governance therefore:

- listens to signals from practice,

- adjusts decision structures accordingly,

- and maintains alignment between responsibility and authority over
  time.

### Avoiding governance drift

Just as responsibility can drift, so can governance. Policies may remain
formally intact while actual practice diverges. New tools may be adopted
without revisiting existing decision structures. Informal norms may
replace documented processes.

Strong co-agency counteracts governance drift by keeping decision-making
visible and discussable. When humans remain active agents rather than
passive operators, misalignments between governance and practice surface
earlier and can be addressed deliberately.

## 

## Co-Agency as connective tissue

Ethics without agency is aspiration. Governance without agency is
bureaucracy. Co-agency is what allows both to function in practice.

By integrating co-agency with ethics and governance, organisations move
beyond compliance and control toward **capability and confidence**.
Decisions remain human-centred even as AI systems become more capable.
Responsibility is exercised rather than assigned. Learning is embedded
rather than episodic.

This integration is what allows AI capability to scale without
fragmenting.

The following chapter turns from integration to application, examining
how co-agency is enacted differently across education, research, and
organisational contexts---while remaining grounded in the same
underlying principles.

# Applying Co-Agency in Context

Human--AI co-agency is not enacted in the abstract. It takes shape
through concrete decisions, institutional norms, and professional
responsibilities that vary across contexts. While the underlying
principles of co-agency remain consistent---clarity of roles, retained
human judgement, accountable decision-making---the way these principles
are expressed differs significantly between education, research, and
organisational or public service settings.

This section explores how co-agency operates in each of these contexts,
highlighting both its distinctive challenges and its unifying function.
The aim is not to prescribe uniform solutions, but to show how co-agency
can be interpreted and enacted in ways that respect contextual purpose
while maintaining capability coherence.

## Education

### Teaching, Assessment, and Student Agency

In education, co-agency is inseparable from questions of learning,
authorship, and trust. Educational institutions are not only users of
AI; they are formative environments in which learners develop norms
about knowledge, judgement, and responsibility. As a result, failures of
co-agency in education have consequences that extend beyond immediate
outcomes, shaping how future professionals understand their own agency
in relation to technology.

### Teaching and pedagogical judgement

In teaching contexts, AI systems are increasingly used to support lesson
planning, content generation, feedback design, and learning analytics.
Co-agency here requires clarity about where AI supports pedagogical
judgement and where it must not substitute for it.

Educators retain responsibility for:

- defining learning outcomes,

- interpreting student needs,

- and exercising professional judgement about what constitutes
  meaningful learning.

When AI tools are treated as authoritative sources rather than supports,
pedagogical agency erodes. Teaching risks becoming content delivery
rather than relational, interpretive practice. Co-agency preserves the
educator's role as designer and judge, with AI operating as an assistive
resource rather than a pedagogical actor.

### Assessment and academic judgement

Assessment is one of the most sensitive sites of co-agency in education.
Decisions about grading, progression, and feedback carry significant
consequences for learners. Introducing AI into assessment workflows
without clear co-agency risks undermining both fairness and legitimacy.

Healthy co-agency in assessment requires:

- explicit boundaries on where AI may assist (e.g. rubric alignment,
  feedback drafting),

- retained human authority over evaluative judgement,

- and transparent communication with students about AI's role.

Over-automation in assessment can produce the appearance of objectivity
while concealing interpretive choices embedded in systems. Conversely,
blanket prohibition often drives AI use underground, eroding trust.
Co-agency enables a middle path: AI supports assessment work without
displacing accountability.

### Student agency and learning integrity

Co-agency in education is not limited to staff. Students are also agents
interacting with AI systems, often with fewer resources and less
guidance. Institutions that focus solely on staff use while ignoring
student agency create asymmetries that undermine integrity.

Supporting student co-agency involves:

- helping learners understand when AI can support learning and when it
  undermines it,

- modelling reflective use rather than surveillance-based control,

- and recognising that students' agency develops through guided
  practice, not prohibition.

When students are treated only as compliance subjects, capability
development is stunted. When they are treated as developing agents,
co-agency becomes part of professional formation rather than a
disciplinary issue.

## Research

### Authorship, Interpretation, and Methodological Integrity

In research contexts, co-agency is tightly coupled to epistemic
responsibility. Research is not only about producing outputs, but about
justifying claims, interpreting evidence, and contributing to shared
knowledge. AI systems can influence each of these stages, often subtly.

### Authorship and contribution

One of the most visible co-agency challenges in research concerns
authorship. AI tools can generate text, suggest structures, summarise
literature, and propose interpretations. Without clear co-agency,
responsibility for scholarly claims becomes blurred.

Co-agency requires that:

- human researchers remain accountable for intellectual contributions,

- AI use is disclosed where relevant,

- and authorship is not conflated with output production.

The core question is not whether AI was used, but **who is responsible
for the reasoning and claims presented**. Co-agency preserves the
integrity of authorship by ensuring that AI assistance does not obscure
responsibility.

### Interpretation and epistemic judgement

Research depends on interpretation: deciding what findings mean, how
evidence supports claims, and where uncertainty remains. AI systems may
assist with pattern detection or synthesis, but they do not understand
context, theory, or consequence.

Failures of co-agency in research often occur when AI outputs are
treated as interpretations rather than inputs. This can lead to
overconfidence, methodological shortcuts, or unexamined bias.

Strong co-agency ensures that:

- AI-supported analysis is subject to critical scrutiny,

- interpretive authority remains human,

- and uncertainty is acknowledged rather than smoothed over.

This is particularly important in interdisciplinary research, where
assumptions embedded in AI systems may not align with domain-specific
epistemologies.

### Methodological integrity over time

AI tools evolve rapidly, and methods that appear robust today may be
problematic tomorrow. Co-agency supports methodological integrity by
embedding reflection and review into research practice.

Rather than fixing rules about permissible tools, co-agency focuses on:

- ongoing evaluation of how AI affects research design,

- shared learning across research groups,

- and adaptive governance that responds to emerging norms.

In this way, co-agency supports innovation without compromising rigour.

## Organisations and Public Service

### Operational Decisions and Leadership Responsibility

In organisational and public service contexts, co-agency is most
directly linked to accountability and legitimacy. AI-supported decisions
can affect employment, access to services, resource allocation, and
public trust. Here, the stakes of agency misalignment are particularly
high.

### Operational decision-making

AI is increasingly used to support operational decisions:
prioritisation, risk assessment, forecasting, and case handling.
Co-agency requires that these systems do not become de facto
decision-makers.

Healthy co-agency ensures that:

- humans understand how AI outputs are generated,

- decision boundaries are clearly defined,

- and overrides are legitimate and supported.

When AI outputs are treated as defaults rather than recommendations,
responsibility quietly shifts from people to systems. This shift is
rarely explicit, but it has profound implications for accountability.

### Leadership and responsibility

Leadership plays a central role in establishing co-agency. Leaders set
expectations about whether AI use is reflective or performative, whether
questioning outputs is encouraged or discouraged, and whether
responsibility is owned or displaced.

Capability-oriented leadership:

- models critical engagement with AI,

- supports staff who raise concerns or escalate uncertainty,

- and resists narratives of inevitability or replacement.

Where leadership abdicates agency---by deferring to vendors, tools, or
external pressure---co-agency collapses throughout the organisation.

### Public trust and legitimacy

In public service contexts, legitimacy depends on visible
accountability. Citizens must be able to understand who is responsible
for decisions that affect them, even when AI systems are involved.

Co-agency supports legitimacy by:

- keeping decision authority human and identifiable,

- enabling explanation and contestation,

- and embedding learning from impact and feedback.

Without co-agency, AI use may appear efficient but opaque, eroding trust
even when outcomes are technically sound.

## A shared capability, differently expressed

Across education, research, and organisational contexts, the expression
of co-agency varies. What remains constant is its function: **to keep
humans accountable, judgement-bearing, and capable of learning in
AI-supported systems**.

Co-agency is not a constraint on innovation. It is what allows
innovation to persist without eroding responsibility. It is not an
abstract ethical ideal, but a practical capability that shapes how
decisions are made, justified, and revised over time.

The final chapter turns inward, focusing on reflection and renewal---how
co-agency itself must be revisited, evaluated, and sustained as
technologies and contexts evolve.

# Reflection and Renewal

Human--AI co-agency cannot be established once and then assumed to
persist. It is shaped continuously by changes in technology,
organisational context, professional norms, and social expectations.
Systems that appear well-designed at one moment may quietly drift at
another. Practices that feel responsible in early experimentation may
become risky once scaled. Roles that were once clear may blur as tools
become more capable and more embedded.

For these reasons, reflection and renewal are not optional additions to
co-agency. They are the mechanisms through which co-agency is sustained.

Reflection allows individuals and organisations to notice how agency is
actually operating in practice, rather than how it is assumed to
operate. Renewal translates that noticing into adjustment: revisiting
boundaries, updating norms, and re-asserting human responsibility where
it has weakened or become unclear.

This chapter focuses on two complementary aspects of that work:
reflective prompts that support inquiry at individual and collective
levels, and the conditions required to sustain healthy co-agency over
time.

## Reflective Prompts for Individuals and Teams

Reflection in the context of co-agency is not about self-assessment or
compliance. It is about sense-making. It creates space to ask whether
responsibility, judgement, and authority are aligned in lived practice,
not just in policy or design.

The prompts below are not intended to be answered definitively. Their
value lies in being revisited periodically, particularly after moments
of change, uncertainty, or tension.

### Reflection on judgement and decision-making

- When AI systems contribute to decisions in my work, how do I
  understand my own role? Do I feel like the decision-maker, the
  reviewer, or the executor of an output?

- Are there situations where I defer to AI outputs more than I am
  comfortable admitting? What pressures or assumptions make that
  deferral feel necessary?

- When I disagree with an AI-supported recommendation, do I feel
  authorised and supported to challenge it?

These questions help surface whether judgement is being actively
exercised or quietly displaced. Discomfort, hesitation, or ambiguity are
signals worth attending to rather than suppressing.

### Reflection on responsibility and accountability

- If an AI-supported decision led to harm or scrutiny, would it be clear
  who is accountable?

- Do accountability structures in my context match actual decision
  authority, or do they assign responsibility without agency?

- Where do I rely on policy, process, or technology to carry
  responsibility that I am unwilling or unable to hold myself?

These prompts reveal whether responsibility is genuinely owned or
rhetorically assigned. Persistent misalignment between accountability
and authority is one of the strongest indicators of fragile co-agency.

### Reflection on learning and adaptation

- How do we currently learn from AI use in our team or organisation?

- Do we treat unexpected outcomes as failures to hide, or as signals to
  examine?

- When practices change, do we understand why, or do changes happen by
  drift?

Reflection without learning leads to stagnation. Learning without
reflection leads to repetition. Co-agency depends on both.

### Reflection on culture and norms

- What behaviours are implicitly rewarded or discouraged in relation to
  AI use?

- Is questioning AI outputs seen as diligence or resistance?

- Do we make space for uncertainty, or do we expect confidence even when
  understanding is partial?

Cultural signals often shape agency more powerfully than formal
guidance. These prompts help surface whether the environment supports
thoughtful engagement or quiet compliance.

Used collectively, these reflections support shared understanding. Used
individually, they help professionals retain agency even within complex
systems. In both cases, reflection is a practice of responsibility, not
introspection for its own sake.

## 

## Sustaining Healthy Co-Agency

Reflection alone does not sustain co-agency. Renewal requires action:
adjustments to structures, norms, and expectations that keep agency
aligned with responsibility over time.

Healthy co-agency is not static. It must be actively maintained against
several predictable pressures.

### Pressure toward automation and convenience

As AI systems become more capable and more integrated, the temptation to
offload judgement increases. What begins as assistance can gradually
become default authority, particularly when systems are reliable most of
the time.

Sustaining co-agency requires deliberate friction:

- moments where humans must interpret rather than accept,

- expectations that outputs are questioned rather than consumed,

- and visible endorsement of human judgement, even when it slows
  processes.

Convenience is not neutral. Left unchecked, it erodes agency.

### Pressure toward diffusion of responsibility

In complex systems, responsibility often becomes diluted. Multiple
actors touch a decision, but no one feels fully accountable. AI systems
can intensify this diffusion by acting as intermediaries that obscure
where choices are made.

Healthy co-agency resists diffusion by:

- clarifying who decides, who reviews, and who learns,

- making responsibility explicit rather than assumed,

- and revisiting these clarifications as systems evolve.

Responsibility must be repeatedly re-claimed, not assumed to persist.

### Pressure toward performative compliance

As governance and ethical expectations increase, organisations may
respond by producing policies, statements, or assurances that are weakly
connected to practice. Co-agency collapses when responsibility becomes
performative rather than enacted.

Renewal involves reconnecting formal structures to lived decisions:

- testing whether governance supports or constrains judgement,

- checking whether ethical commitments shape real choices,

- and adjusting where gaps appear.

Capability is revealed in practice, not documentation.

### Supporting renewal through structure

Sustaining co-agency benefits from structural support:

- regular moments of review built into workflows,

- forums where AI use can be discussed without defensiveness,

- and leadership behaviours that legitimise questioning and escalation.

Without these supports, reflection becomes individualised and fragile.
With them, co-agency becomes collective and resilient.

### Leadership and renewal

Leaders play a decisive role in sustaining co-agency. Not by issuing
directives, but by modelling how responsibility is held.

Leadership that supports co-agency:

- acknowledges uncertainty,

- treats reflection as strength rather than hesitation,

- and demonstrates willingness to revise decisions.

Where leaders abdicate agency---by deferring to tools, vendors, or
inevitability narratives---co-agency weakens throughout the system.

### Co-agency as a commitment, not a state

Ultimately, sustaining healthy co-agency requires a shift in how success
is understood. Success is not the absence of friction, nor the seamless
integration of AI into workflows. Success is the ongoing capacity to
notice, question, and adapt.

Co-agency is not something an organisation *has*. It is something an
organisation *does*, repeatedly.

It is renewed whenever:

- a human pauses to interrogate an output,

- a team revisits a boundary that no longer holds,

- or an institution chooses accountability over convenience.

This book has argued that co-agency is central to AI capability because
it keeps humans answerable for decisions in environments shaped by
increasingly powerful systems. Reflection and renewal are what keep that
answerability alive.

There is no final configuration in which co-agency is complete. There is
only continued attention, exercised judgement, and the willingness to
adapt responsibly as conditions change.

That ongoing work is not a limitation. It is the condition under which
AI remains a tool for human purposes rather than a force that quietly
reshapes them.

## Sustaining Co-Agency Over Time

Human--AI co-agency is not something an organisation can design once and
then assume will endure. It is shaped continuously by technological
change, organisational pressure, professional norms, and cultural
signals about authority and responsibility. Even well-designed co-agency
arrangements can erode quietly if they are not revisited and reinforced.

Throughout this book, co-agency has been framed not as a technical
safeguard, but as a practical capability: one that determines whether AI
augments human judgement or gradually displaces it. The distinction
matters. When co-agency is healthy, responsibility is visible, judgement
is exercised confidently, and learning is possible. When it weakens,
accountability becomes diffuse and decision-making loses legitimacy.

Co-agency is sustained through attention rather than control. It is
renewed whenever individuals feel authorised to question AI outputs,
when escalation is treated as responsible practice rather than failure,
and when organisations choose clarity over convenience. These moments
are rarely dramatic, but they are decisive.

There is no final or optimal configuration of co-agency. What counts as
appropriate human involvement will vary by context, risk, and maturity,
and will need to be recalibrated as systems and practices evolve. The
goal is not to minimise automation or maximise oversight, but to ensure
that agency remains aligned with responsibility.

This book should therefore be read as a guide for ongoing work rather
than a set of rules. Its purpose is to help readers notice when agency
is drifting, articulate where responsibility truly lies, and intervene
deliberately before erosion becomes normalised.

Co-agency is not something an organisation has. It is something an
organisation does---again and again---as AI becomes more capable and
more persuasive. Sustaining that practice is what keeps AI accountable
to human purposes rather than quietly reshaping them.
